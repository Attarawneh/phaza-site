Evaluating Arabic language models

A single number on an Arabic benchmark tells you almost nothing about whether a system will work in Amman, Riyadh or Casablanca. This note sets out how Phaza measures Arabic models: what we test, how the tests are built, who judges the answers, and what we will and will not publish.

Summary

Arabic is not one language problem. It is a formal written standard used across twenty-two countries, a family of spoken varieties that differ enough to break a model trained on the standard alone, and a habit of switching into English or French mid-sentence. A model can score well on an aggregate Arabic benchmark and still fail the first week of a deployment, because the benchmark averaged away the exact conditions that deployment runs in.

Phaza therefore evaluates by country, variety, register, domain and channel, reports results per cell rather than as a headline, and treats any score produced without a stated protocol as an unverified claim — including our own.

What an aggregate score hides

Four properties of Arabic break the assumption that one score describes capability:

  • Diglossia. The written standard and the spoken variety coexist in the same institution, often in the same conversation. A model fluent in one may be unreliable in the other, and an averaged score cannot show it.
  • Distance between varieties. Maghrebi and Gulf speech differ more than some pairs of European languages that nobody would evaluate as one. Collapsing them into “dialect” measures nothing a buyer can act on.
  • Morphology and orthography. Rich inflection, a root-and-pattern system, absent short vowels, and inconsistent spelling of dialect and of borrowed terms mean that surface string matching misjudges answers that are correct, and accepts answers that are not.
  • Code-switching. In Gulf enterprise and North African service interactions, switching is not noise — it is the register. Filtering it out of evaluation produces a system that scores well and fails live.

The evaluation matrix

Every Phaza evaluation is specified along the same five axes used to specify a dataset, so a result can be traced back to the evidence it was trained on:

  • Variety — Modern Standard Arabic, country variety, and regional variation inside a country.
  • Register — formal, professional, conversational, informal, user-generated.
  • Domain — government, finance, health, energy, logistics, legal, retail, industrial.
  • Channel — document, chat, voice, search, form, image, multimodal.
  • Language interaction — Arabic only, Arabic–English, Arabic–French, and the mixing patterns of the market in question.

A result is reported per cell of that matrix. A model that is strong in MSA government correspondence and weak in Moroccan voice is described that way, because that is the sentence a deployment decision actually depends on.

Task families

  • Comprehension and reasoning — questions whose answers are verifiable, in the variety and domain being tested.
  • Instruction following — whether the system does what was asked, in the register it was asked in, including politeness and address conventions that differ by country.
  • Grounded answering — retrieval over institutional documents, scored on whether the answer is supported by the cited source rather than merely plausible.
  • Extraction — names, places, institutions, amounts and dates as they are actually written locally.
  • Speech — recognition and understanding under the acoustic conditions of the real channel, not studio audio.
  • Documents and vision — regional forms, layouts, handwriting and signage.
  • Safety and cultural judgement — refusals, sensitive subjects and local appropriateness, judged in-region rather than against a translated policy.

How test sets are built

Held out, and kept out

Evaluation data is created and held separately from training data, under separate access. It is never used for training, fine-tuning or model selection. A set that has been trained on is no longer an evaluation set, and we retire it rather than quietly reuse it.

Written by people who speak the variety

Items are authored and reviewed by native speakers of the specific variety under test, working in-region, under the consent and compensation rules described in our privacy policy. Translated test items are marked as translated, because a translated question measures translation as much as it measures the model.

Gold answers and adjudication

Each item carries an expected outcome and a scoring rubric written before any model is run. Items are scored independently by more than one reviewer; disagreements are adjudicated by a third, and the rate of disagreement is recorded and reported. An item that reviewers cannot agree on is a defective item, not a hard one, and it is rewritten or removed.

Contamination control

Before a set is used, it is checked against the training corpus for overlap, and newly written items are preferred over material that has circulated publicly. Where a public benchmark is used for comparability, we say so, and we treat its numbers as indicative rather than decisive, because we cannot prove what any model has seen.

How runs are conducted

  • One protocol per comparison. Every model in a comparison is run by us, under the same prompts, the same decoding settings, the same context limits and the same scoring code. We do not place our numbers next to numbers published by someone else under unknown conditions.
  • Fixed and recorded. Prompts, settings, seeds, dates, checkpoint identifiers and hardware are recorded with the result. A result without them is not a result.
  • Repeated. Generative tasks are run multiple times; we report the spread, not only the best pass.
  • Blind where judgement is involved. Human reviewers do not know which system produced an answer, including when one of them is ours.
  • Failures kept. Cases where our own model loses are retained and used as the brief for the next data collection round. That loop is the reason the evaluation exists.

What we publish, and what we will not

When Phaza publishes a result for Salam or for any other system, it will carry: the checkpoint and its configuration, the date, the full protocol, the per-cell results including the weak cells, the disagreement rate between reviewers, and the limitations we know of. Enough for a competent team to argue with us.

We will not publish:

  • a score without the protocol that produced it;
  • a comparison against a model we did not run ourselves under the same conditions;
  • a projected, extrapolated or partial-checkpoint number presented as a measurement;
  • a headline average that conceals the cells where the system is weak.

This note exists before any Salam results are published on purpose. The method should be inspectable before the numbers arrive, so that the numbers can be judged against a standard we committed to in advance rather than one assembled afterwards to fit them.

Limitations of this note

  • It describes Phaza's own practice. It is not a standard, and we do not claim it as one.
  • Human judgement remains judgement. Adjudication and blind review reduce bias; they do not remove it.
  • Coverage is uneven. Varieties with less available material are harder to test well, and we would rather say so than imply parity we have not earned.
  • Evaluation measures what it was built to measure. A system can pass every cell here and still meet something nobody specified.
